# The proposal as a working instrument

Part VI of the Article proposes conditions for accountable restoration. This page sets them out as a
**restoration schedule** that can be attached to an approval, a restoration plan or a funding agreement. Every
element comes from the Article's text, and the schedule takes its force from the instrument that imposes it.

## A. Instrument and decisions governed

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Legal instrument | The approval, environmental permit, restoration plan, funding agreement or public-works contract that imposes the terms, and the provision that authorizes each term |
| Decisions governed | Permission to alter the site · release of funds · acceptance of completed work · commencement of each damaging phase · discharge of continuing responsibility |
| Missing powers | Where existing authority is inadequate, the specific power that is missing |
| Role of the project | Repair, or compensation relied on to permit new loss. Compensation triggers the credit accounting of section C |

## B. Baseline and diagnosis

| Item | Record |
| --- | --- |
| What is degraded, and for whom | Present functions as well as historical extent. A former mangrove may now be a productive pond or a feeding flat |
| Options compared | Protection without engineering · continued pond management · hydrological reconnection · planting · mixed intervention · alternative location |
| Diagnostic record | Tidal restriction · elevation and datum · sediment and water quality · propagule supply · species composition · the surrounding habitat mosaic |
| The whole habitat account | Mangrove, mudflat, pond and other vegetation recorded together, so that a gain in canopy is not booked while open flat is lost |
| Future conditions | Sea level, sediment supply and space for habitat migration |
| Control of infrastructure | Who controls embankments, drainage structures, gates and access routes, and whether each has agreed to operate them |
| Proportionality | A small reversible trial proceeds on a limited baseline with explicit stop conditions. Compensation for permanent loss needs stronger evidence of replaceability |

## C. Credit accounting, when restoration is used as currency

A compensation credit has four properties, and each has a regulatory counterpart.

| Property of a credit | Regulatory counterpart | Where it is already law |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When it will be redeemed | Temporal loss, answered by ratios or discounts | 33 C.F.R. § 332.3(f)(2), (m); Fla. Admin. Code r. 62-345.600 |
| The chance it will not be redeemed | Likelihood of success | 33 C.F.R. § 332.3(f)(2); the risk score of r. 62-345.600 |
| The security behind it | Financial assurances and funded correction | 33 C.F.R. § 332.3(n) |
| The schedule of draws | Release of credits against demonstrated performance | 33 C.F.R. § 332.8(o)(8)(i) |

**The discount.** Florida divides the functional gain a mitigation project is expected to produce by the product of
a time-lag factor and a risk score. The share of a promised gain that is credited:

| Time lag | Time-lag factor | Share credited at risk score 1.25 | At risk score 2.0 | At risk score 3.0 (high risk) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 years | 1.14 | 70% | 44% | 29% |
| 6 to 10 years | 1.25 | 64% | 40% | 27% |
| 11 to 15 years | 1.46 | 55% | 34% | 23% |

A promise is credited at face value in one circumstance: the replacement is in place, and working, before the
loss.

**The schedule states, for each damaging phase:**

- which functions must be **available before the phase begins**, and which may recover later under a supported
  interim arrangement;
- the lag between loss and replacement, the risk score, and the credit that results;
- where a function is slow or impossible to replace in the location, the response: a smaller footprint, a
  different sequence, an alternative site, or no credit;
- the limit of ratios: multiplying area cannot supply missing land control, repair an unsuitable water regime or
  replace a function that depends on its location.

## D. Two kinds of acceptance

| | Engineering completion | Ecological acceptance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is checked | Controllable work: grading, channels, culverts | Trajectories: inundation and connectivity, then recruitment, then the functions chosen in diagnosis, such as open-flat availability or bird feeding |
| When | At completion | Over time, with stated observation frequency and seasonal coverage |
| Standard | Specification | A reference condition and a plausible range, justified for the site before work begins |
| Who decides changes | The contract | A named authority |
| Payment | The contractor is paid for verified work | An identified manager holds a funded obligation to monitor and correct |

Between the two, a proportionate retention, a funded maintenance account or a public budget commitment carries the
project, as legal authority and project risk allow. Withholding all payment until the ecosystem matures transfers
uncontrollable risk to contractors, and paying everything at completion leaves correction unfunded.

## E. Correction and residual risk

| Item | Specify |
| --- | --- |
| Failed milestone | Who reviews it, how quickly the cause is investigated, and which options are available: maintenance for a blocked culvert, redesign for poor site selection, abandoning a planting area for habitat conflict |
| Default response | A diagnosis before any replanting, which can repeat the original error |
| Responsibility | Negligent or noncompliant conduct, agreed ecological uncertainty and exceptional events, each treated separately; maintenance duties; event reporting; conditions for release |
| Financing | Private projects: security that is legally available against insolvency or abandonment. Public projects: an earmarked budget and a successor manager. Full expected costs of land, design, monitoring, delay and correction |
| Additionality | Existing obligations and their actual funding kept separate from proposed incremental work |

## F. People and evidence

| Item | Specify |
| --- | --- |
| Who is affected | Pond operators, tenants, neighbors, conservation managers, people who rely on flood control |
| When they participate | Before restoration fixes land and water use |
| What participation is tied to | Checking the baseline · identifying seasonal access or drainage problems · reviewing alternatives · challenging a reported milestone · requesting investigation of a failure |
| Monitoring information | Intelligible enough to contest, with the responsible authority and the response process named |
| Cross-border tasks | Advance notification, compatible measurements, a shared baseline, and an agreed authority to respond to each exceedance |

## The timing norm in each jurisdiction

The sequence the schedule requires is already written in each legal system the Article compares.

| Jurisdiction | Text |
| --- | --- |
| United States | Compensation "in advance of or concurrent with" the impact, and additional compensation for temporal losses (33 C.F.R. § 332.3) |
| Florida | No time lag only when mitigation offsets the impact at or before the time of impact (Fla. Admin. Code r. 62-345.600) |
| Hong Kong | The Technical Memorandum prefers mitigation before the works, and the planning guidelines for Deep Bay measure loss in area and in function |
| Mainland China | "Compensate first, occupy later" in the State Council's 2016 plan, and replacement of equivalent area and quality under art. 21 of the Wetland Protection Law |

The reform gives these principles a decision to govern: the point at which a damaging phase may begin.

## What any functional standard discloses

Endpoint · baseline · sampling effort · spatial interpretation. A short-lived concentration of birds in stocked
ponds may be redistribution, and not added regional carrying capacity.
