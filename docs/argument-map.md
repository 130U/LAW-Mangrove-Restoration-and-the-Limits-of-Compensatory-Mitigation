# Argument map

The Article's argument Part by Part: each claim, the authority or evidence it rests on, the strongest objection to
it and the response. Authorities are cited in full in the notes of the
[Article](../manuscript/mangrove-restoration-and-the-limits-of-compensatory-mitigation.md).

## Thesis

Restoration enters law in two roles. As repair it is an end in itself. As currency it pays for permission to
destroy something else, and a compensation credit is what the word says: a credit, to be discounted for delay and
default, secured, and released against performance. Restoration objectives should therefore be tied to
identifiable decisions and allocated responsibilities, and a promise of future function should be credited in full
only when the replacement exists before the loss.

## Part II. Comparing legal decisions and ecological outcomes

| Claim | Authority | Objection | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Functions are compared, and not counts of statutes | Van Hoecke on comparative method | A specialized mangrove law means stronger protection | Specialized laws can create exemptions, and general laws can contain specific duties |
| Protection, repair and compensation are distinct | 33 C.F.R. § 332.1; the 2008 mitigation rule | Restoration is restoration | Compensation follows avoidance and minimization |
| Attribution needs an explicit evidentiary chain | Duty, decision, conduct, site conditions, response, function | Trees after a law prove the law worked | Each observation supports one link, and each claim has its own counterfactual |
| A baseline cannot assume deterioration that law already forbids | Comparison with the lawful alternative | Without the project the site would degrade | Preventing that degradation may already be a legal duty |
| Restoration as repair and restoration as currency warrant different scrutiny | Maron et al. on the conditions for offsetting; the National Research Council's 2001 review; 33 C.F.R. §§ 332.3, 332.8 | A good project is a good project | Repair issues no claim on anything and can be tried on modest evidence. Compensation issues a credit, whose value depends on when it is redeemed, on the chance of default, on the security behind it and on the schedule of draws |

## Part IV. Restoration evidence and its limits

| Claim | Evidence | Objection | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrological rehabilitation is credible where tidal restriction limits recovery | West Lake: reference elevations, tidal creeks, natural recruitment | West Lake proves Florida's law works | Recovery began before the 1996 statute, and the project was mitigation for development elsewhere |
| Recovery is multidimensional | Lewis and Gilmore; Roberts | No significant difference in fish means equivalence | Nonsignificance is not equivalence, and fish are not every function |
| A plan is not proof of performance | The 2008 West Lake mitigation plan | The plan required monitoring | Permit histories, accounts, monitoring and release decisions are the evidence |
| More mangrove can mean less mudflat and fewer birds | Sung et al.: in Deep Bay mangrove cover rose from 8 to 15 percent and other vegetation from 3 to 14 percent while mudflat fell from 56 to 43 percent | Mangroves caused the bird declines | Causation is not shown. A scheme that measured compensation in canopy would still have recorded the first of those changes as a gain |
| Two sites cannot isolate the effect of law | Jia et al., Futian and Mai Po | Protection explains expansion | Sedimentation, planting, colonization and urban pressure are confounded |

**Florida's arithmetic applied to San Tin's timetable.** The share of a promised gain that the rule credits is the
reciprocal of the product of the time-lag factor and the risk score.

| Time lag | Time-lag factor | Share credited at risk score 1.25 | At risk score 2.0 | At risk score 3.0 (high risk) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 years | 1.14 | 70% | 44% | 29% |
| 6 to 10 years | 1.25 | 64% | 40% | 27% |
| 11 to 15 years | 1.46 | 55% | 34% | 23% |

On no row is the promise credited in full.

## Part VI. Conditions for accountable restoration

| Condition | Core requirement |
| --- | --- |
| A. Existing instruments, specified decisions | Commitments attach to the approval, plan or funding agreement, and are tied to permission, release of funds, acceptance, damaging phases and discharge |
| B. A defensible baseline | What is degraded and for whom; protection, pond management, reconnection, planting, mixed and alternative sites compared |
| C. Engineering completion separate from ecological acceptance | Different milestones, justified ranges, seasonal coverage, a named authority for changes, and functions available before each damaging phase |
| The timing norm | Already written in each legal system compared: federal rules on advance or concurrent compensation, Florida's discount, Hong Kong's Technical Memorandum and planning guidelines, and mainland policy and art. 21. The reform gives these principles a decision to govern: the point at which a damaging phase may begin |
| D. Correction and residual risk | Who reviews failure, how fast, which options, what funding |
| E. Affected people tied to evidence and decisions | Pond operators, tenants and residents involved before land and water use is fixed |

## Part VII. Costs, measurement and enforcement

| Objection | Response |
| --- | --- |
| Delay prolongs degradation | Data are reused, monitoring is targeted and requirements are stated in advance. For irreversible loss, delay is weighed against the risk that the replacement never arrives |
| Existing law already requires plans | Then the question is how existing duties affect approval, acceptance and continuing liability |
| Canopy is easier to administer | It is used where it matches the objective, and a limited set of justified endpoints keeps obligations reviewable |
| Conditions go unimplemented | Requirements need a reviewer, resources and consequences |
| How would anyone know it works? | Records of initial condition, duties, funding, land control, interventions and outcomes, with matched sites |

## Conclusion

Proportionate experimentation is appropriate in degraded habitats. Stronger justification is owed when a
restoration promise supports irreversible loss. Florida's most transferable lesson is arithmetic: its assessment
method discounts a promised function for the years it will take to arrive and the chance that it will not, and
credits it in full only when the replacement exists before the loss.
